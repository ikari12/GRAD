\section*{Abstract}
% =====================================================================
\noindent\textbf{Purpose:} The speed-based Decoupling Index (DI $=$ HR/speed)
is displayed to millions of endurance athletes by GPS-watch platforms
(e.g., TrainingPeaks, Strava) as a marker of aerobic durability, yet its
sensitivity to route terrain has never been quantified. We aimed to
(1)~quantify the route-geometry artifact in field-based DI, (2)~test
whether a regression-based Gradient-Adjusted Cardiac Drift (GACD)
correction removes it, and (3)~derive the number of sessions required
for reliable individual profiling.

\noindent\textbf{Methods:} We analysed $N = 13{,}750$ real-world workouts
from $K = 675$ users in the public FitRec (Endomondo) dataset. A
controlled simulation ($N = 5{,}000$ synthetic workouts, cardiac drift
fixed to zero, all parameters drawn from the data) established the
theoretical artifact ceiling. Route predictability of DI versus
GACD-derived metrics was assessed by group $k$-fold cross-validated
$R^2$. Reliability was quantified by intraclass correlation
(ICC(1,1)), route-matched ICC, and Spearman--Brown (SB) convergence
across aggregated sessions.

\noindent\textbf{Results:} With zero cardiac drift, route geometry alone
produced DI from 0.70 to 1.40 (cross-validated $R^2 = 0.80$). In real
data, route features predicted DI with $R^2 = 0.49$ (0.72 on hilly
terrain); after GACD correction, predictability was eliminated
($R^2 \approx 0.02$). Day-to-day (Occasion) variance dominated all
metrics (56--84\%). Gradient sensitivity from GACD reached adequate
reliability (SB $\geq 0.80$) at $\geq 5$ aggregated sessions.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions:} Speed-based DI is heavily contaminated by
route geometry, and 42\% of routes mechanically produce DI $< 1.0$
without any cardiac drift. GACD removes this artifact, and multi-session
aggregation ($\geq 5$ sessions) yields a reliable individual durability
profile for field monitoring.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} aerobic decoupling; durability; cardiac
drift; gradient correction; reliability; wearable monitoring

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Aerobic decoupling---the progressive rise in heart rate (HR) relative to
pace or power during prolonged exercise---is widely used as a field-based
indicator of endurance fitness.\textsuperscript{1,2} Consumer platforms
such as TrainingPeaks and Strava present the Decoupling Index (DI) to
millions of athletes and apply Friel's $<5\%$ threshold\textsuperscript{2}
to signal an adequate aerobic base. This threshold has never been
empirically validated in a peer-reviewed study, and---critically---the
metric is almost universally computed as HR/speed on consumer GPS
watches, not as the HR/power ratio assumed in the physiological
literature.\textsuperscript{3}

On variable terrain, speed is determined primarily by gradient, so
speed-based DI necessarily carries a route-geometry component. Consider a
front-loaded course: an athlete who climbs early and descends late will
record a high HR/low speed in the first half and a low HR/high speed in
the second half, mechanically producing DI $< 1.0$---the appearance of
``negative drift''---even with a perfectly stable cardiovascular system.
What has not been established is (a)~the \emph{magnitude} of this
component, (b)~whether it \emph{dominates} the physiological signal in
real-world data, and (c)~what signal \emph{remains} once it is corrected.

The most closely related large-scale work, Smyth et al.\ in 82,303
marathon runners,\textsuperscript{4} established that decoupling magnitude
and onset predict performance and confirmed the ecological validity of
field-based decoupling measurement at scale, but did not examine the
route-geometry artifact, the dimensional structure of multiple decoupling
indices, or the variance attributable to person, route, and occasion. The
reliability of field-based HR metrics has long been a
concern,\textsuperscript{5,6} and the IOC consensus\textsuperscript{7}
emphasises the external-load/internal-response distinction. Yet no study
has quantified how much of the variance in HR-response metrics is
attributable to the person, the route, and the transient daily state
(occasion)---the very partition that determines how many sessions a
practitioner must average before a value is trustworthy.

Recent physiological work has framed endurance ``durability'' as a
multi-dimensional construct. Meixner et al.\textsuperscript{3} proposed
four conceptual constructs (durability, fatigability, repeatability,
resilience), and the accompanying commentaries\textsuperscript{8,9} and
Jones' resilience proposal\textsuperscript{10} reflect an active,
unresolved debate over whether these constructs are empirically
separable. Whether such constructs are separable when operationalised
with the \emph{speed-based} field metrics that athletes actually use is
unknown; we treat this dimensionality question as a secondary,
exploratory analysis and focus the present study on the measurement
problem that has immediate practical consequences. To bridge theory and
field data, we introduce three author-defined, speed-based
operationalizations---the Decoupling Index (DI; durability), the Fading
Index (FI; fatigability), and the Resilience Index (RI; resilience)---and
note that these are original operationalizations, not definitions taken
from Meixner et al.\textsuperscript{3}

\noindent\textbf{Aims and hypotheses.} This study addresses three primary
aims, with associated hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{A1}] Quantify the route-geometry artifact in speed-based
  DI. \emph{(H1: route features predict a substantial fraction of DI
  variance; simulation with zero drift reproduces the observed DI range.)}
  \item[\textbf{A2}] Test whether a within-workout GACD regression removes
  the artifact. \emph{(H2: DI route predictability is high but GACD-derived
  route predictability is near zero.)}
  \item[\textbf{A3}] Derive the number of sessions required for adequate
  individual reliability. \emph{(H3: transient Occasion variance exceeds
  50\%, so multi-session aggregation is required for SB $\geq 0.80$.)}
\end{itemize}
Secondary/exploratory analyses examine (E1)~the empirical dimensionality
of the three speed-based indices, and (E2)~whether the temporal placement
of descent relates to cardiac drift.

\section{Methods}
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Data and filtering}
The publicly available FitRec dataset,\textsuperscript{11} comprising
253,020 GPS-tracked Endomondo workouts, was used. Each workout record $j$
contains irregularly time-stamped arrays of heart rate
($\mathrm{HR}_{j,i}$, optical wrist sensor, bpm), speed ($v_{j,i}$,
GPS-derived, km$\cdot$h$^{-1}$), altitude ($a_{j,i}$, m), and geographic
coordinates, where $i = 1,\dots,N_j$ indexes data points. Where the
recorded speed array was missing or shorter than 10 points, speed was
derived from GPS coordinates via the Haversine formula (values
$>150$~km$\cdot$h$^{-1}$ excluded as outliers). The cohort is 94.6\% male,
4.8\% female, 1.2\% unknown; age, body mass, and training history are
unavailable.

A four-step inclusion pipeline was applied: (1)~HR, altitude, and speed
arrays each required $N_j \geq 30$ finite points (a minimum for stable
within-half mean estimation with $\geq 15$ points per half), with missing
values linearly interpolated between flanking valid points; (2)~total
duration $T_j = t_{N_j}-t_1 > 90$~min, ensuring sufficient time for
cardiac drift to manifest;\textsuperscript{1} (3)~altitude range
$\Delta a_j = \max_i a_i - \min_i a_i > 200$~m (median retained $= 349$~m),
and, for suspected loop routes ($>80\%$ overlap), a start-to-end altitude
deviation $|a_{N_j}-a_1| < 50$~m to control barometric drift; (4)~each
temporal half (H1: $i \leq \lfloor N_j/2\rfloor$; H2: $i > \lfloor
N_j/2\rfloor$) required $>5$ points with $v_i > 0.5$~km$\cdot$h$^{-1}$
(i.e., $\geq 6$ per half). When speed was entirely unavailable, an
HR-only DI ($=\bar{\mathrm{HR}}_{\mathrm{H2}}/\bar{\mathrm{HR}}_{\mathrm{H1}}$)
was computed as a fallback; FI and RI required speed.

This yielded $N = 13{,}750$ workouts from $K = 675$ users for Study~1
(cycling 61.8\%, running 16.9\%, mountain biking 16.7\%, other 4.6\%;
median duration 181~min). Studies~2--3 required $\geq 5$ repeated workouts
per user, yielding a high-engagement subset of $N = 2{,}343$ workouts from
$K = 314$ users (cycling 83.9\%, running 7.6\%, mountain biking 5.8\%,
other 2.7\%; median duration 208~min [IQR 152--298]).

\subsection{Speed-based index definitions}
Speed in the raw data is in km$\cdot$h$^{-1}$ (indices 1--3). For the
within-workout regression (indices 4--6), speed was converted to
m$\cdot$s$^{-1}$ ($\div 3.6$).

\textbf{(1) Decoupling Index (DI).} Let $\mathcal{S}_h = \{i \in
\mathrm{H}_h : v_i > 0.5~\mathrm{km{\cdot}h^{-1}}\}$ for $h\in\{1,2\}$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DI} = \frac{\bar{\mathrm{HR}}_{\mathcal{S}_2}\,/\,\bar{v}_{\mathcal{S}_2}}
                     {\bar{\mathrm{HR}}_{\mathcal{S}_1}\,/\,\bar{v}_{\mathcal{S}_1}},
  \label{eq:di}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\mathrm{HR}}_{\mathcal{S}_h}=|\mathcal{S}_h|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_h}\mathrm{HR}_i$
(analogously for $\bar v$). DI $>1$ indicates cardiac drift.

\textbf{(2) Fading Index (FI).} Point-to-point gradient was computed from
cumulative horizontal distance $D_i=\sum_{l=1}^{i} v_l\,\Delta t_l/3600
\times 1000$~(m) (minimum horizontal step 0.1~m), with $g_i=\mathrm{clip}
(100\times\Delta a_i/\Delta D_i, -50, +50)\%$. The gradient range was
partitioned into five bins $\mathcal{B}_b$: $[-50,-10)$, $[-10,-3)$,
$[-3,3)$, $[3,10)$, $[10,50)\%$ (flat $<3\%$; moderate 3--10\%; steep
$>10\%$):
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FI}_b=\frac{\bar v_{\mathcal{S}_2\cap\mathcal{B}_b}}{\bar v_{\mathcal{S}_1\cap\mathcal{B}_b}},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{FI}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}^*|}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}^*}\mathrm{FI}_b,
  \label{eq:fi}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{B}^*=\{b:|\mathcal{S}_1\cap\mathcal{B}_b|>3 \text{ and }
|\mathcal{S}_2\cap\mathcal{B}_b|>3\}$. FI was defined for 13,742 workouts.

\textbf{(3) Resilience Index (RI).} Altitude was smoothed by a trailing
moving average (window $w=\max(1,\lfloor N/20\rfloor)$). A climb was
initiated when smoothed altitude rose $>0.5$~m between points and
terminated when this ceased; only climbs with cumulative vertical gain
$>50$~m were retained (informed by cycling-industry
convention\textsuperscript{12}). For retained climbs $C_1,\dots,C_m$ with
$m\geq2$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RI}=\bar v_{C_m}/\bar v_{C_1},\qquad
  \bar v_{C_l}=|\{i\in C_l:v_i>0.5\}|^{-1}\!\!\sum_{i\in C_l,\,v_i>0.5}\!\! v_i.
  \label{eq:ri}
\end{equation}
Workouts with $m<2$ received no RI. RI is a preliminary proxy for the
resilience construct;\textsuperscript{10} it does not control the relative
steepness or temporal position of climbs, and is not directly comparable
across workouts with different $m$.

\subsection{Within-workout regression: Gradient-Adjusted Cardiac Drift}
For indices 4--6, speed was in m$\cdot$s$^{-1}$ and gradient was:
\begin{align*}
  g_i &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(100\times\frac{a_{i+1}-a_i}{d_i},-50,+50\right),\\
  d_i &= \max(v_i\,\Delta t_i, 0.1~\mathrm{m}),\quad
  \Delta t_i=\max(t_{i+1}-t_i,0.1~\mathrm{s}).
\end{align*}
All variables were evaluated at midpoints ($\mathrm{HR}^*_i=(\mathrm{HR}_i+
\mathrm{HR}_{i+1})/2$, etc.). On the subset of valid indices ($|\mathcal{V}|
\geq 20$), ordinary least squares (OLS) was fitted:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{HR}^*_i = \beta_0 + \beta_{\mathrm{grad}}\,g_i
  + \beta_{\mathrm{speed}}\,v^*_i + \beta_{\mathrm{time}}\,t^*_i
  + \varepsilon_i,\qquad
  \hat{\bm\beta}=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y.
  \label{eq:gacd}
\end{equation}
This yields (4)~GACD ($\hat\beta_{\mathrm{time}}$,
bpm$\cdot$min$^{-1}$)---within-session HR drift after removing terrain and
pace confounds; (5)~gradient sensitivity ($\hat\beta_{\mathrm{grad}}$,
bpm$\cdot$\%$^{-1}$); (6)~speed sensitivity ($\hat\beta_{\mathrm{speed}}$,
bpm$\cdot$(m$\cdot$s$^{-1}$)$^{-1}$). Coefficients (5)--(6) are interpreted
as route-corrected, submaximal fitness-profiling coefficients.
Point-level gradients were clipped to $\pm 50\%$. Multicollinearity was
minimal (median VIF: gradient 2.01, speed 2.09, time 1.07; 99.8\% of
sessions VIF $<10$, 92.4\% below 5).\textsuperscript{13} $\hat\beta_{\mathrm{time}}$
is related to, but distinct from, the between-session durability construct
of Maunder et al.,\textsuperscript{14} which concerns fatigue-induced
shifts in the power--duration relationship.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis: temporal autocorrelation}
Point-level GPS data exhibit temporal autocorrelation, violating the OLS
independence assumption. On a random subsample ($N = 300$; median 499
points) the GACD regression was re-run and three diagnostics computed.
The \textbf{Durbin--Watson} statistic $DW=\sum_{t=2}^{n}(e_t-e_{t-1})^2/
\sum_{t=1}^{n}e_t^2$ had a median of 0.355 (IQR 0.245--0.501), with 100\%
of workouts showing strong positive autocorrelation ($DW<1.5$). The
lag-1 \textbf{autocorrelation} of residuals was high (median $\rho_1=0.803$,
IQR 0.735--0.862), implying an AR(1) effective-sample-size reduction of a
median 89\% ($n_{\mathrm{eff}}/n=0.109$). \textbf{Newey--West} HAC standard
errors (Bartlett kernel, $L=\lfloor 4(n/100)^{2/9}\rfloor$) were
$\approx 2\times$ larger than OLS SEs (median ratio: gradient 2.03, time
2.12, speed 2.06); under strong persistence this is a lower
bound.\textsuperscript{15} Crucially, OLS point estimates
$\hat{\bm\beta}$ remain unbiased; only SEs and per-workout $R^2$ are
affected. Because the study's conclusions rest on workout-level and
person-level aggregates (one scalar per workout) rather than point-level
inference, they are not mechanistically affected by within-workout
autocorrelation.

\subsection{Estimated-power comparison}
As an alternative correction, DI was recomputed replacing speed with
estimated metabolic power $P_i=v_i\,C_w(g_i)$~(W$\cdot$kg$^{-1}$), where
$C_w(g)$ is the Minetti\textsuperscript{16} 5th-order walking-cost
polynomial $C_w(i)=280.5\,i^5-58.7\,i^4-76.8\,i^3+51.9\,i^2+19.6\,i+2.5$
($i$ = gradient as a fraction, valid for $i\in[-0.45,+0.45]$, floored at
0.1~J$\cdot$kg$^{-1}\cdot$m$^{-1}$):
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DI}_{\mathrm{EstP}}=\frac{\bar{\mathrm{HR}}_{\mathcal{S}_2}/\bar P_{\mathcal{S}_2}}
                                    {\bar{\mathrm{HR}}_{\mathcal{S}_1}/\bar P_{\mathcal{S}_1}}.
  \label{eq:estp}
\end{equation}
Extreme outliers were removed by 1st/99th percentile
trimming\textsuperscript{17} ($N=13{,}080$). The Minetti function was
derived for walking/running; applying it to a cycling-dominated dataset
introduces a bioenergetic mismatch (see Discussion).

\subsection{Study 1 --- Quantifying and correcting the artifact (A1, A2)}
\textbf{Route predictability.} Thirteen route features (total
ascent/descent, altitude range, max/min altitude, gradient mean/SD,
climb/descent/flat fractions, ascent-front and descent-front ratios,
duration) were used. Within each CV fold, features were $z$-standardised
using training-fold statistics only, and RidgeCV
($\alpha\in\{0.01,0.1,1,10,100\}$, internal LOO-CV) and gradient-boosted
trees (100 trees, depth 3) were fitted. Generalisation was assessed by
group $k$-fold cross-validated $R^2$ ($k=\min(5,K_{\mathrm{valid}})$; all
workouts of a user in the same fold):
\begin{equation}
  R^2_{\mathrm{CV}}=1-\frac{\sum_{f}\sum_{j\in\mathrm{test}_f}(y_j-\hat y_j^{(f)})^2}
                          {\sum_{f}\sum_{j\in\mathrm{test}_f}(y_j-\bar y)^2}.
  \label{eq:cvr2}
\end{equation}
The hilly subset was $\Delta a_j>400$~m. For GACD the target was the
within-person deviation $\tilde y_{kj}=y_{kj}-\bar y_{k\cdot}$ (users with
$\geq 3$ workouts), isolating route effects from person means.

\textbf{Controlled simulation.} $N=5{,}000$ synthetic workouts
($N_{\mathrm{pts}}=60$, split at 30) were generated with cardiac drift set
\emph{exactly} to zero; all parameters were drawn from FitRec. Route types
were sampled with empirical proportions (from
\texttt{abc\_metrics.csv}, $N=2{,}343$): front-climb (ascent-front $>0.6$;
42.2\%): $g_{\mathrm{H1}}\sim\mathcal N(+1.0,1.84^2)$,
$g_{\mathrm{H2}}\sim\mathcal N(-1.0,1.84^2)$; back-climb (ascent-front
$<0.4$; 10.2\%): reversed; symmetric (47.6\%):
$g\sim\mathcal N(0,1.84^2)$. Physiological parameters were sampled from
FitRec-derived distributions: $\mathrm{HR}_0\sim U(107,159.5)$,
$\gamma_{\mathrm{HR}}\sim\max(0.1,\mathcal N(1.91,1.48^2))$~bpm/\%,
$v_0\sim\max(1.0,\mathcal N(1.91,0.44^2))$~m/s,
$\gamma_v\sim\max(0.01,\mathcal N(0.27,0.15^2))$~(m/s)/\%. The
data-generating process was
$\mathrm{HR}_i=\mathrm{HR}_0+\gamma_{\mathrm{HR}}g_i+\epsilon^{\mathrm{HR}}_i$
($\epsilon^{\mathrm{HR}}\sim\mathcal N(0,3^2)$) and
$v_i=\max(0.5, v_0-\gamma_v g_i+\epsilon^v_i)$
($\epsilon^v\sim\mathcal N(0,0.3^2)$). No time-dependent term was
included, so any non-unity DI arises purely from gradient asymmetry.

\textbf{Descent-placement analysis (E2).} With descent-front ratio
$\delta=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{H1}}|\min(\Delta a_i,0)|/\sum_{i}|\min(\Delta a_i,0)|$,
$\delta$ was tested as a predictor of GACD via (a)~between-person Pearson
$r$ (and controlling for total descent); (b)~within-person $r$ for users
with $\geq 5$ varied routes ($n=108$); (c)~steepness stratification
($\sigma_g<5$; 5--8; $>8$); (d)~sport subgroups. All eight exploratory
$p$-values were BH-FDR adjusted.\textsuperscript{18}

\subsection{Study 2 --- Variance structure and reliability protocol (A3)}
\textbf{ICC(1,1).} One-way random-effects ANOVA\textsuperscript{19} for
users with $n_k\geq5$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ICC}(1,1)=\frac{\mathrm{MS_B}-\mathrm{MS_W}}
                        {\mathrm{MS_B}+(n_0-1)\mathrm{MS_W}},
  \label{eq:icc}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{MS_B}=\sum_k n_k(\bar x_{k\cdot}-\bar x_{\cdot\cdot})^2/(K-1)$,
$\mathrm{MS_W}=\sum_k\sum_j(x_{kj}-\bar x_{k\cdot})^2/(N-K)$, and
$n_0=(N-\sum_k n_k^2/N)/(K-1)$. ICC(1,1) was selected because workouts are
unique, non-crossed samples per user.

\textbf{Variance partitioning.} $\%\mathrm{Person}=\mathrm{ICC}\times100$.
Within-person deviations were predicted from the 13 route features
(RidgeCV, group $k$-fold CV), yielding $R^2_{\mathrm{route}}$; since this
operates only on within-person variance $(1-\mathrm{ICC})$:
\begin{align*}
  \%\mathrm{Route}&=\max(0,R^2_{\mathrm{route}})\cdot(1-\mathrm{ICC})\cdot100,\\
  \%\mathrm{Occasion}&=100-\%\mathrm{Person}-\%\mathrm{Route}.
\end{align*}
$\%\mathrm{Occasion}$ captures daily state, environment, sensor noise, and
unmodelled route variation. Uncertainty was estimated by user-level
bootstrap ($B=500$).

\textbf{Route-matched sensitivity.} Workout pairs were matched if
$|(\Delta a_j-\Delta a_{j'})/\Delta a_j|\leq0.20$ and
$|(A^+_j-A^+_{j'})/A^+_j|\leq0.20$ ($A^+$ = total ascent; $n=451$ pairs,
95 users). The $\pm20\%$ tolerance accommodates GPS altitude uncertainty.
Route-matched ICC provides an upper-bound Person estimate.

\textbf{Split-half and convergence.} For users with $\geq 10$ workouts,
Pearson $r$ between chronological halves was Spearman--Brown corrected:
$\mathrm{SB}=2r_{\mathrm{sh}}/(1+r_{\mathrm{sh}})$. For $k=2,\dots,10$,
users with $\geq 2k$ workouts were selected, workouts randomly permuted,
and the first/second $k$ used to compute $\mathrm{SB}_k$, estimating the
reliability achievable from $k$ randomly sampled sessions. ICC was also
recomputed for $n_{\min}\in\{3,5,8,10,15\}$, and convergent validity
assessed against user-level median speed.

\subsection{Study 3 --- Dimensionality (secondary, E1)}
Person-level medians of DI, FI, RI were computed for users with $\geq 5$
workouts. Pairwise Pearson correlations were bootstrapped ($B=2{,}000$,
users resampled). PCA on column-standardised medians used parallel
analysis\textsuperscript{20} for factor retention (observed eigenvalues
vs.\ the 95th percentile of $B=1{,}000$ permutations). A robustness check
varied $T_{\min}$ (60--120~min) and $\Delta a$ (100--300~m) across 25
combinations.

\subsection{A1: The route-geometry artifact dominates speed-based DI}
The mean DI across all workouts was 0.92 (Table~\ref{tab:descriptive}),
indicating that HR/speed was, on average, \emph{lower} in the second
half---an apparent negative cardiac drift. This counterintuitive result
is explained by route geometry. Classifying routes by ascent-front
fraction revealed three distinct DI distributions (Supplementary
Figure~S1): front-climb routes (ascent-front $>0.6$; $N=975$, 42.4\%) had
a median DI of 0.770 (fast descent in the second half mechanically
depresses DI); symmetric routes ($0.4\leq$ ascent-front $\leq0.6$;
$N=1{,}090$, 47.5\%) a median of 0.978; and back-climb routes
(ascent-front $<0.4$; $N=232$, 10.1\%) a median of 1.157.

The controlled simulation confirmed the mechanism directly: with cardiac
drift fixed to zero, route geometry alone produced DI values from 0.70 to
1.40, with cross-validated $R^2 = 0.80$
(Figure~\ref{fig:simulation}A)---the theoretical ceiling of the artifact.
In real data, route features predicted naive DI with cross-validated
$R^2 = 0.49$ (all routes) and $0.72$ (hilly subset). Thus a majority of
the variance in the metric that millions of athletes interpret
physiologically is, in fact, terrain. A direct practical corollary is that
athletes on front-loaded hilly routes routinely observe DI $<1.0$ and may
mis-interpret a geometric artifact as an excellent aerobic base.

\subsection{A2: GACD removes the artifact and isolates the signal}
Applying the GACD regression (Eq.~\ref{eq:gacd}) eliminated route
predictability: within-person GACD deviations were essentially
unpredictable from route features (cross-validated $R^2\approx+0.02$;
Figure~\ref{fig:simulation}B), reducing the artifact from the empirical
$0.49$ and the theoretical $0.80$ to near zero (H2 confirmed). Unlike
commercial Grade-Adjusted Pace algorithms, which correct pace for gradient
but ignore time-dependent cardiac drift, GACD addresses both confounds
simultaneously.

Replacing speed with estimated metabolic power (Eq.~\ref{eq:estp}) did
\emph{not} achieve this. DI/estimated-power showed a route dependency of
comparable magnitude but opposite sign ($r=-0.46$ vs.\ $+0.49$ for
DI/speed; cross-validated $R^2=0.20$ and 0.20, respectively), worsening on
hilly terrain ($r=-0.56$, $R^2=0.31$ vs.\ DI/speed $r=+0.46$, $R^2=0.19$).
The reversal arises because the Minetti cost function drops on descents
($C_w\approx1.30$~J$\cdot$kg$^{-1}\cdot$m$^{-1}$ at $-8\%$, minimum
$\approx0.94$ near $-15.4\%$, vs.\ 2.5 at 0\%), inflating the denominator
ratio on early-descent routes; the walking-derived cost function is also
mismatched to a cycling-dominated dataset. The data-driven GACD regression
is therefore the preferred correction, because it learns the
modality-specific submaximal relationship directly from each workout.

\subsection{A3: Occasion dominates variance; $\geq 5$ sessions restore
reliability}
Day-to-day Occasion variance dominated every metric (56--84\%;
Table~\ref{tab:main}; H3 confirmed), which explains why single-session
field values are unreliable. Crucially, this within-person variability is
\emph{manageable} by aggregation. Route-matched analysis
($n=451$ matched pairs) showed that a substantial part of the apparent
Occasion variance is actually route-related and controllable: holding
route approximately constant raised Person estimates for GACD
($0.22\rightarrow0.47$), gradient sensitivity ($0.38\rightarrow0.56$), and
speed sensitivity ($0.44\rightarrow0.74$)---18--31 percentage points.

\subsection{Secondary (E1): dimensionality is, at most, two constructs}
DI and FI were strongly negatively correlated ($r=-0.60$, 95\% CI
$[-0.69,-0.49]$; bootstrap $B=2{,}000$), consistently across cycling
($-0.62$), running ($-0.50$), and mountain biking ($-0.58$): both indices
capture the same gradient-response coupling that GACD removes. PCA
retained one factor; the second eigenvalue (1.005) fell marginally below
the parallel-analysis threshold (1.031) by only 0.026 units
(Table~\ref{tab:pca}), providing \emph{fragile} support for a
two-construct organisation. RI was largely independent (DI--RI $r=+0.01$;
FI--RI $r=+0.12$) but unreliable (ICC $= 0.10$), so its apparent
independence may reflect measurement noise rather than construct
distinctness. The DI--FI redundancy was stable across 25 threshold
combinations ($\bar r=-0.60$, SD $= 0.02$, range $[-0.63,-0.57]$). This
dimensionality result is exploratory and should be treated with caution;
bootstrap confidence intervals for eigenvalues were not computed.

\section{Discussion}
% =====================================================================
This study delivers three practically consequential findings. First, the
speed-based DI that millions of athletes rely on is dominated by route
geometry: with cardiac drift fixed to zero, terrain alone explains 80\% of
DI variance, and in real data it explains roughly half (0.49; 0.72 on
hilly terrain). A direct corollary is that 42\% of routes---front-climb
profiles---mechanically produce DI $< 1.0$ regardless of fitness. Athletes
on hilly terrain who see DI $< 1.0$ are therefore likely mis-interpreting
a geometric artifact as evidence of an excellent aerobic base. To our
knowledge this is the first quantification of a systematic bias in a
metric already in daily use at population scale, and it extends Smyth et
al.\textsuperscript{4} by identifying the route-geometry component as a
major confound beyond the road-marathon context.

Second, the artifact is correctable with a simple per-session linear
model. GACD reduces route predictability from 0.49 (empirical) and 0.80
(theoretical ceiling) to $\approx 0.02$, isolating the physiological drift
signal. Notably, the seemingly more principled alternative---converting
speed to estimated metabolic power via a published cost
function---\emph{over}-corrects and reverses the bias, because a rigid,
walking-derived cost function cannot adapt to mixed-modality field data.
The advantage of GACD is precisely that it learns each individual's
submaximal HR--gradient--speed relationship from the signal itself, making
it a general tool for isolating cardiac drift from any terrain-confounded
ratio. The key contribution is therefore not contingent on the
speed-vs-power distinction.

Third, and most important for practitioners, the variance structure
dictates the measurement protocol. Occasion accounts for 56--84\% of
variance---not a nuisance to be minimised, but the central quantitative
argument for aggregation, and quantitative support for the IOC
consensus\textsuperscript{7} principle that external load $\neq$ internal
response. Controlled laboratory settings suppress day-to-day variation by
design, creating the illusion that one session suffices; the ecological
scale here exposes the true session-to-session fluctuation practitioners
face. The route-matched residual (26--54\%) was not explained by effort
intensity ($r = -0.004$, ns), likely reflecting unmeasured readiness
factors (sleep, hydration, temperature, accumulated
load).\textsuperscript{6} Gradient sensitivity emerges as the best single
candidate: it is the only metric whose route-corrected within-person
deviations remain positively predictable, it correlates most strongly with
external speed, and it reaches SB $\geq 0.80$ at $\geq 5$ aggregated
sessions. The preserved rank-order consistency (large ICC--SB gap) means
that longitudinal \emph{trends} across training blocks are interpretable
even when individual sessions vary.

\textbf{A dual-profiling framework.} The three GACD coefficients play
distinct roles: $\beta_{\mathrm{time}}$ indexes within-session drift
(durability), whereas $\beta_{\mathrm{grad}}$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{speed}}$
are route-corrected fitness-capacity profiles. $\beta_{\mathrm{time}}$ is
related to, but distinct from, the between-session durability construct of
Maunder et al.,\textsuperscript{14} which concerns fatigue-induced shifts
in the power--duration relationship. The recommended protocol is:
(1)~apply GACD per session to remove route-geometry artifacts,
(2)~extract gradient and speed sensitivity as the durability profile, and
(3)~aggregate $\geq 5$ sessions, using sport-specific baselines.

\textbf{On dimensionality.} The strong DI--FI redundancy and the fragile
two-construct PCA result are consistent with a property of the specific
speed-based operationalizations, not a theoretical refutation of the
multi-construct framework;\textsuperscript{3,8,9} both DI and FI respond
to the same gradient asymmetry that GACD removes. This dimensionality
analysis is exploratory and is not the central claim of the paper.

\textbf{Limitations.} (1)~DI here is HR/speed, not HR/power; HR/speed is a
weaker proxy for cardiac efficiency in cycling---where power, drag, and
rolling resistance intervene---than in running. GACD absorbs terrain
variance but not residual aerodynamic/mechanical confounds. Future work
should restrict primary analyses to running or develop a cycling-specific
cost model. (2)~The dataset is cycling-dominated (61.8\% overall; 83.9\% of
the high-engagement subset), and the running subsample there is too small
($N = 47$ workouts, 3 users with $\geq 5$ sessions) for stable estimation;
the $\geq 5$-session recommendation should be considered primarily
cycling-specific pending running-specific validation. (3)~Secondary data
with uncontrolled, wrist-optical HR sensors of variable
accuracy;\textsuperscript{6,22,23} sensor noise likely inflates Occasion
variance. (4)~No gold-standard criterion (VO$_2$max, power). (5)~94.6\%
male, limiting generalisability, particularly given documented sex
differences in cardiovascular drift. (6)~Point-level temporal
autocorrelation (median DW $= 0.355$, lag-1 ACF $= 0.803$) means
per-workout inference (not point estimates) is unreliable and per-workout
$R^2$ is an upper bound; GLS was not applied. (7)~The two-construct
conclusion rests on a fragile 0.026-unit eigenvalue margin and is
exploratory. (8)~RI (ICC $= 0.10$) is unreliable. (9)~Exploratory findings
were not pre-registered; 7/8 tests survived BH-FDR but this does not
account for broader analytic flexibility. (10)~Observational, single
dataset.

% =====================================================================
\section{Practical Applications}
% =====================================================================
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Do not interpret raw DI on hilly terrain.} A DI $< 1.0$ on
  a front-loaded (climb-early, descend-late) course is most likely a
  route-geometry artifact, not evidence of superior aerobic fitness. On
  variable terrain the Friel $<5\%$ rule of thumb should not be applied to
  uncorrected, speed-based DI.
  \item \textbf{Apply GACD before interpreting drift.} Fitting a simple
  per-session linear model of HR on gradient, speed, and time removes the
  terrain confound and isolates the physiological drift
  ($\beta_{\mathrm{time}}$). This is more robust than converting speed to
  an estimated-power cost function, which over-corrects on descents.
  \item \textbf{Aggregate at least five sessions.} Because day-to-day
  variation dominates single sessions (56--84\%), average $\geq 5$
  sessions of gradient sensitivity (or $\geq 3$ of speed sensitivity)
  before drawing conclusions about an athlete's durability profile.
  \item \textbf{Track trends, not single values.} Rank-order stability is
  preserved across sessions, so within-athlete trends across training
  blocks are interpretable even when absolute session values fluctuate.
  \item \textbf{Use sport-specific baselines.} Gradient sensitivity
  differs markedly across modalities (cycling $+2.11$, MTB $+1.17$,
  running $+0.47$~bpm$\cdot$\%$^{-1}$), so comparisons should be made
  within sport.
\end{itemize}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
% =====================================================================
Speed-based aerobic decoupling, as displayed to millions of athletes, is
heavily contaminated by route geometry---up to 80\% of its variance under
controlled conditions and roughly half in the field---so that a large
fraction of hilly-route DI values are geometric artifacts rather than
physiological signal. A simple within-session Gradient-Adjusted Cardiac
Drift regression removes this artifact (route predictability
$0.49/0.80\rightarrow0.02$) with minimal multicollinearity, and yields
route-corrected profiling coefficients. Because transient day-to-day state
dominates single-session variance (56--84\%), reliable individual
profiling requires multi-session aggregation: gradient sensitivity reaches
adequate reliability (SB $\geq 0.80$) at $\geq 5$ sessions. Together these
provide an actionable, field-deployable protocol for durability monitoring
on variable terrain.
